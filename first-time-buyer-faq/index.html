How it fits together

How assistance “stacks” — and where it can't

Think of a purchase as three layers. Assistance fills the middle layers — but each program only fits certain first mortgages and certain addresses, which is exactly where buyers get tripped up.

Layer 1 · Your first mortgage

The loan that buys the house

FHA at 3.5% down, conventional at 3% down (HomeReady / Home Possible / standard 97% LTV), VA or USDA at 0% down for eligible buyers. Your credit, income, and debts decide this layer first — assistance comes second.

Layer 2 · Assistance

A second loan, grant, or forgivable loan

A silent second from the city, county, CalHFA, or GSFA sits behind the first mortgage and covers down payment and/or closing costs. Most LA-area seconds are deferred — no monthly payment — and are repaid, often with shared appreciation, when you sell or refinance.

Layer 3 · Your own cash

What's left is your cash to close

Your required contribution (often about 1% of price on city/county programs), any closing costs not covered, plus prepaid taxes and insurance. Seller credits negotiated in the offer can shrink this layer — one reason your agent and lender have to plan together.

The two stacking rules that matter most: (1) City vs. County is geography, not a menu. LIPA/MIPA are only for homes inside Los Angeles city limits; HOP is only for unincorporated county areas and participating cities. The same home can never use both. (2) CalHFA's own programs don't mix: MyHome cannot be combined with Dream For All. Beyond those, whether programs stack depends on both programs' written rules and the lender — get the combination confirmed by a participating lender before you write an offer around it.

Before you tour a single home

Pre-approval: what it is, and what to bring

A pre-qualification is a guess based on what you tell a lender. A pre-approval is verified — income, assets, employment, and credit actually checked — and it's what LA sellers expect to see attached to your offer. Gather these first and the process speeds up by weeks.

Documents to gather

  • Recent pay stubs
  • Bank statements (typically 2 months)
  • Employment history
  • Previous tax returns (typically 2 years)
  • Photo ID
  • Statements for any debts or other income

The same starter list CalHFA publishes for contacting a loan officer.

What the lender checks

  • Credit scores from all three bureaus (your middle score is often the working number)
  • Debt-to-income ratio — your debts vs. gross income
  • Stable, documentable income
  • Funds for your contribution and reserves
  • Which assistance programs your file fits

Straight answers

First-time buyer FAQ

The questions Los Angeles first-time buyers actually ask — answered the way Daniel answers them on a call, including the parts that aren't good news.

The down payment

How much down payment do I really need to buy a home in Los Angeles?

You do not need 20%. Eligible first-time buyers commonly buy with 3% down on a conventional loan (Fannie Mae HomeReady, Freddie Mac Home Possible, or a standard 97% LTV loan) or 3.5% down on an FHA loan with a credit score of 580 or higher. Down payment assistance can cover part or all of that down payment for buyers who qualify. Twenty percent down avoids mortgage insurance, but it is not a requirement to buy.

On a $650,000 home, that's the difference between saving $130,000 (20%) and $19,500–$22,750 (3%–3.5%) — before assistance. Try the estimator above with your own price.

Can I really buy a home with only $500 to $5,000 out of pocket?

It is possible in specific situations, and it is the range Daniel's first-home page describes for clients who have used low-down-payment paths. The pattern is usually: a lower-priced home or condo, assistance covering the down payment, a small required buyer contribution (several city and county programs require about 1% of the price from the buyer's own funds), and seller or lender credits helping with closing costs.

The honest limits: it is not typical at every price point, it depends on the program's current funding, and it is never guaranteed. Most buyers should budget for their contribution plus closing costs, and confirm their real cash-to-close figure with a lender before they start touring.

Do I need 20% down to avoid mortgage insurance?

On a conventional loan, private mortgage insurance (PMI) is generally required below 20% down and can typically be removed once you reach about 20% equity. FHA loans charge mortgage insurance premiums (MIP) instead, which work differently and often last for the life of the loan at minimum down payment. Avoiding mortgage insurance is a real benefit of a larger down payment — but for many LA buyers, waiting to save 20% costs more in rent and price movement than PMI ever would. That's a math conversation, not a rule.

What are closing costs, and how much are they beyond the down payment?

Closing costs are the fees and prepaids charged to complete the purchase, separate from the down payment: lender origination and processing fees, appraisal, credit report, title insurance, escrow fees, recording charges, and prepaid property taxes and homeowners insurance.

As a planning estimate in California, buyers often budget roughly 2% to 5% of the purchase price, though the real figure varies by price, lender, loan type, and property. Some assistance can be applied to closing costs, and seller credits negotiated in your offer can offset part of them. Your lender's Loan Estimate — which you receive early in the process — replaces every estimate on this page.

Assistance programs

What is down payment assistance, and is it free money?

Down payment assistance (DPA) is a second loan, grant, or forgivable loan from a state, county, city, or authority program that helps cover your down payment and sometimes closing costs. Most of it is not free money: deferred second loans are typically repaid when you sell, refinance, or transfer the home, and several Los Angeles programs also share in the home's appreciation. A smaller number of options are grants or loans forgiven after a set period. The terms — not the headline amount — are what matter.

What down payment assistance programs are available in Los Angeles and California?

The main ones, compared in the table above: CalHFA MyHome (statewide, up to 3.5% FHA / 3% conventional), GSFA Platinum (statewide, up to 5%, not limited to first-time buyers), City of LA LIPA (up to $161,000) and MIPA (up to $115,000) for homes inside city limits, and LA County HOP80/HOP150 (up to $100,000) outside city limits. CalHFA Dream For All (up to 20%, capped at $150,000) is real but its 2026 application window closed March 16, 2026 — watch CalHFA for a future round rather than planning around it today.

Can down payment assistance programs be combined or stacked?

Sometimes, but not in every combination. City of Los Angeles programs (LIPA/MIPA) and LA County's HOP can never combine on the same home — they cover different geography. CalHFA states MyHome cannot be combined with Dream For All. Other combinations depend on both programs' written rules and the lender's participation. Any stack has to be verified by a participating lender before you rely on it — see how stacking works.

Do I have to pay back down payment assistance?

Usually yes, on the program's terms. The city (LIPA/MIPA), county (HOP), and CalHFA MyHome loans have no monthly payments, but the balance comes due when you sell, transfer title, repay the first mortgage, or reach the end of the term — and the city and county programs also take a share of the home's appreciation. Some GSFA Platinum options are forgivable over time. Read the repayment and shared-appreciation terms before you sign, not after: assistance lowers your cash in, it doesn't lower the home's price.

Who counts as a first-time homebuyer?

For most programs in this guide, a first-time homebuyer is someone who has not owned and occupied a home as a principal residence in the past three years. Owned a home four years ago? You may qualify as a first-time buyer again. Dream For All additionally requires at least one borrower to be a first-generation buyer. Each program's exact definition controls, and your lender verifies it.

Credit & qualifying

What credit score do I need to buy a first home?

It depends on the loan and the program:

  • FHA (HUD floor): 580+ for 3.5% down; 500–579 requires 10% down.
  • Conventional: generally 620 minimum.
  • CalHFA via MyHome: generally 640 for the FHA path, 660–680 for conventional depending on income.
  • GSFA Platinum: 640 minimum.
  • City of LA LIPA / MIPA: 660 minimum middle score.

One caution: lenders can set stricter requirements (“overlays”) than the program floor, so a score that qualifies on paper can still be declined by a specific lender — and approved by another.

My credit is below those numbers. Should I just wait?

Not necessarily — but be honest about which problem you're solving. If your score is below 580, the realistic work is credit repair first: paying down revolving balances, fixing reporting errors, and letting on-time payments age usually moves a score faster than any shortcut, and Daniel can talk through what lenders actually look at. If you're in the 580–660 band, FHA and some assistance paths may already be open to you. What doesn't work is touring homes before a lender has seen your file — the pre-approval conversation is free and tells you exactly where you stand.

What is pre-approval, and how is it different from pre-qualification?

A pre-qualification is a lender's informal estimate based on what you tell them. A pre-approval means the lender has verified your income, assets, employment, and credit and issued a letter for a specific loan amount, subject to the property and final underwriting. Sellers in Los Angeles generally expect a pre-approval letter with an offer, and several assistance programs require pre-approval through a participating lender before funds can be reserved. See the checklist above.

Do I have to take a homebuyer education course?

If you're using most assistance programs, yes. CalHFA requires an approved education and counseling course with a certificate; the City of LA and LA County programs require an 8-hour course from an approved/HUD-approved provider. It's usually inexpensive, available online or in person, and — honestly — useful: it covers exactly the costs and documents this guide summarizes. Complete it early so it never delays a closing.

The process

How long does it take to buy a first home, start to finish?

Preparation and pre-approval often take one to three weeks once documents are gathered, plus any required education course. Home searching varies from days to months. Once an offer is accepted, a California escrow typically runs about 30 to 45 days for inspection, appraisal, underwriting, and closing. Assistance programs with reservation rounds (like the City of LA's) can add weeks before you can even write the offer — another reason to start the lender conversation first. The full sequence is in the timeline above.

I'm buying in Calabasas or the Valley — does assistance still apply?

Statewide programs (CalHFA, GSFA) apply anywhere in California, but every program has price caps and income limits, and Calabasas prices sit far above most of them — LIPA's purchase-price cap, for example, is $956,465 and HOP's caps are $700,000–$850,000, below typical Calabasas single-family prices. For higher-priced areas, the realistic low-down paths are usually the 3%–3.5% loans themselves rather than assistance. If Calabasas is the goal, start with Daniel's Calabasas neighborhood guide on soldbymrsmith.com and a payment conversation — and if you're also selling, his valuation page is the companion on that side.
